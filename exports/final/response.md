When we open the Gospel of Matthew to chapter 14, we meet Jesus at a moment of deep personal grief. He has just learned about the brutal execution of his cousin, John the Baptist. Naturally, Jesus seeks solitude. He steps into a boat to withdraw to a deserted place, needing space to mourn, pray, and breathe.

But the crowds follow him on foot along the shore. 

When Jesus steps off the boat, expecting quiet isolation, he is instead met by thousands of needy, demanding people. Most of us, in our exhaustion or grief, might feel overwhelmed or even resentful. Yet the Gospel tells us that when Jesus saw the crowd, he had compassion on them and healed their sick. His own heartbreak did not close his heart to the heartbreaks of others.

As evening approaches, the disciples encounter a practical crisis. They look at the massive crowd, realize they are in the middle of nowhere, and offer a sensible solution: “Send the crowds away so they can go into the villages and buy food for themselves.” 

It is a response rooted in a mindset of scarcity and reasonable boundaries. The disciples look at the problem, look at their resources, and conclude that it is impossible. 

Then Jesus says something staggering: “They do not need to go away. You give them something to eat.”

Imagine the disciples’ confusion. They search their provisions and bring forward their grand total: five loaves of bread and two fish. In the face of over five thousand people, this offering is almost embarrassingly small. 

Yet Jesus says, “Bring them here to me.”

What follows is a moment rich in Eucharistic language. Jesus takes the bread and fish, looks up to heaven, gives thanks, breaks the loaves, and hands them to the disciples to distribute. 

Notice the pattern: Jesus does not create food out of thin air while keeping his disciples as passive bystanders. He asks them to offer the little they have, he blesses it, and then he uses their hands to distribute the miracle.

Everyone ate and was satisfied. And when they gathered the fragments left over, there were twelve baskets full—one for each of the hesitant disciples who thought they didn't have enough.

This Gospel passage speaks directly to our adult lives, where we so often feel stretched beyond our capacity. We look at the demands of our families, our workplaces, our churches, and a suffering world, and we react like the disciples. We think, "I don't have enough time. I don't have enough patience. I don't have enough energy or faith." We want to send the problems away because our resources feel laughably small.

Jesus does not ask us to possess unlimited resources. He simply asks us not to run away, and to bring him the "five loaves and two fish" of our lives—our limited talents, our imperfect efforts, our weary hearts, and our fragile hope.

When we hold onto our scarcity, it remains small. But when we put our meager offerings into the hands of Christ, he gives thanks, breaks open our potential, and transforms our insufficient "little" into more than enough. 

Whatever situation in your life feels overwhelming or deficient today, bring it to Him. Trust that in the hands of God, what you have is enough to start a miracle.
